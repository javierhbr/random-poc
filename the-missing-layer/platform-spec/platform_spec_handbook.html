DIÁTAXIS TYPE: EXPLANATION

Everyone Finished. Nothing Worked.

A plain guide to the Platform Spec

Why cross-team initiatives fail even when everyone does good work, and how a shared agreement bridges the space between teams without micromanaging code.

The Problem, in One Story

Case Study

A bank decides customers should be able to close their accounts online. It seems simple. Three teams are involved: the Web team builds the screen, the Ledger team handles account state, and the Payments team moves any leftover money.

Three months later, all three report their work complete:

Web team
Built closure flow
done ✓
Ledger team
Built closure state
done ✓
Payments team
Built transfer API
done ✓
The feature: BROKEN
Integration Failure

Then someone tries it:

  • The screen asks the ledger whether the account can be closed — but the ledger's answer doesn't account for pending transactions, because nobody told them it should.
  • The payments team built the transfer as an overnight batch, while the web team assumed it happened instantly and shows a confirmation that isn't true yet.
  • When a test account is closed before its transfer clears, the customer is locked out of their own money.

Nobody did bad work. Each team solved the problem they were given, correctly, inside their own boundary. What was missing was a shared answer to a small number of questions that none of them owned alone.

That shared answer is what a Platform Spec is.

What a Platform Spec Is

Conceptual Boundary

A Platform Spec is the agreement between teams who must change different code to produce one outcome. It says why the change matters, what must be true when it's finished, how the pieces work together, what each team contributes, and what the teams must agree on between them.

What It Deliberately Does NOT Say

It deliberately does not say how any team should build its part. That's the line that makes it useful. The moment a shared document starts prescribing someone's internal design, two things happen: the people who actually know that system stop reading it, and it becomes wrong the first time they learn something.

The Driver & Map Analogy

Think of it as a map agreed between drivers, not a set of turn-by-turn instructions. The map fixes the destination and the rules of the road where paths cross. Each driver picks their own route.

The Three Questions: Why → What → How

Core Narrative Structure

Everything in a Platform Spec hangs off three questions, in order. Skipping any one of them produces a predictable failure:

Skip WHY ➔
Teams optimize the wrong thing beautifully.
Skip WHAT ➔
Everyone builds toward a different finish line.
Skip HOW ➔
Discover mismatches during integration testing.
1. WHY — The Problem, in Three Sentences Strict 3-Sentence Limit

Who has the problem, and what it costs.

"Customers can't close accounts online. It generates around 400 support calls a month, each averaging eleven minutes. New regulation requires a self-service closure path this year."

Three sentences is a genuine limit, not a stylistic preference. If you need more, you've started describing a solution, anchoring everyone before they've understood the problem. The Why lets teams choose sensibly when hitting unanticipated design decisions without convening a meeting.

2. WHAT — The Outcome, Written So Someone Can Watch It Happen Demonstrable

State what is true when finished, in language a non-engineer can verify by looking at a screen:

"A customer can close an eligible account and receive written confirmation, with any remaining balance transferred, within one business day."
❌ "Improve account management experience"
Unverifiable. Nobody can tell when it's done.
❌ "Build the account closure API"
Task wearing an outcome's clothing. Complete API ≠ customer can close account.
3. HOW — The Way the Pieces Work Together Interaction Flow

Describes where the flow starts, who participates, what information moves, and in what order.

1. Customer ➔ requests closure in Account UI
2. Account UI ➔ asks Ledger: "is this account eligible?"
└─ Ledger needs facts from Balance & Compliance
3. Ledger ➔ returns "eligible"
4. Balance ➔ transfers funds, returns receipt
5. Ledger ➔ close ONLY after a receipt exists!
6. Account UI ➔ confirmation to customer

Look at step 5: It's one line, and it's the difference between a working feature and a customer locked out of their money. It isn't a web decision, a ledger decision, or a payments decision. It's a decision about how they relate — which means it has no natural home except this document.

What deliberately stays OUT of the How: Endpoint paths, field names, payload formats, database schemas, class/function names, and deployment settings. All of that belongs to the teams who own the code.

The Part Everyone Underestimates: Agreements

Ownership Matrix

If the three questions are the skeleton, this section is where the document earns its cost: the decisions that no single team can make alone.

THE PLATFORM DECIDES
  • • The shared outcome
  • • Who owns which responsibility
  • • The contracts between components
  • • What happens when something is down
  • • Rules that span more than one component
  • • What "done" means, and who checks
EACH TEAM DECIDES
  • • Its internal design and data model
  • • Its language, framework, code structure
  • • How it tests
  • • How it migrates and rolls out
  • • Its own estimate and plan

Three Agreements Teams Routinely Push Downward:

1. Contracts

The interface belongs to neither side. Agree up front: who provides, who consumes, inputs, outputs, error cases — in plain terms, not wire formats.

2. What Customer Sees When Broken
Compliance down ➔ "Can't process online — call us" (fail closed!)
Balance down ➔ Closure blocked, untouched
Ledger down ➔ "Try again shortly"
3. Rules Spanning Components

"Eligible to close" requires facts from Balance, Ledger, and Compliance.

Decision: Ledger composes eligibility; Balance & Compliance expose facts only.

Contributions Go Two Ways

Feedback Loops

Cross-team work usually fails because a team discovers an assigned task is impossible or built on invalid assumptions. Responsibilities are recorded with a status, and status matters more than anything else in the table:

PROPOSED ACCEPTED TEAM BUILDS
Objected: "We can't transfer synchronously at that latency"

Spec owner amends the spec or writes down why constraint stands ➔ Resets to Proposed.

Objecting isn't obstruction. It's the mechanism by which the spec finds out it was wrong while that's still cheap to fix. When reality contradicts the spec, it changes that day, with a line recording what changed and why. A stale spec is worse than no spec, because people still believe it.

Deliver in Slices, Not in Layers

Roadmap Strategy
BY LAYER (Fails Consistently)
Phase 1: All the screens
Phase 2: All the APIs
Phase 3: All data changes
Phase 4: Connect together ⚠️ (Risks land here!)

Defers every integration question to the end, when schedule has no room left and assumptions have hardened.

BY SLICE (Platform Spec Approach)
Slice 1: See whether account can be closed
Slice 2: Close an empty account
Slice 3: Close one with money in it
Slice 4: Edge cases and bulk

Crosses every layer needed and can be shown working. Every slice includes its own error and empty states!

Three People Who Need Names

Spec Owner

One named person. Keeps document true, resolves objections. Usually whoever most needs the outcome—not necessarily a manager.

Component Owner

One per team. Accepts or objects to responsibility, works out team delivery, gives estimation ranges.

Slice Verifier

One per slice (rotating). The ONLY person who can declare a slice finished by demonstrating it working end-to-end.

Team A: complete | Team B: complete ➔ NOT DONE ❌
Slice Verifier demonstrates working ➔ DONE ✓

The Ways This Goes Wrong

1. The spec grows

Someone adds field names, sequence diagrams, suggested class structures. Teams stop reading it. Cut anything that isn't a decision made here.

2. The spec never changes

Either nothing has been learned in a month (impossible), or people stopped updating it. A stale spec is worse than no spec.

3. Objections stop happening

If no team pushes back, check safety. A spec owner treating objections as authority challenges gets compliance instead of truth.

4. The hardening phase reappears

Error handling drifts to the end of the plan one slice at a time. Watch for it closely.

Two Clarifications Worth Stating Plainly:
  • Count teams, not repositories. One team across four repos coordinates in hallway conversations. Two teams sharing one repo need an agreement. Ownership is the expensive boundary.
  • One new API call between two teams isn't an initiative. Agree inputs/outputs, open two tickets, move on.

How to Draft a Platform Spec in 5 Steps

1
Write 3-Sentence WHY: Who has the problem and what it costs. No solution language.
2
Define Demonstrable WHAT: Write an outcome verifiable by watching a screen.
3
Map HOW Sequence & Ordering Constraints: Step-by-step flow. Highlight constraints no single team owns (e.g., close ONLY after receipt).
4
Record Agreements & Send Proposed Contributions: Assign status PROPOSED until teams accept or object.
5
Define Vertical Slices & Name Slice Verifier: Order demonstrable slices including error states.

What Each Team Writes for Itself (Component Spec)

Once a team accepts a responsibility, they write their own short document. It answers 4 questions and inherits everything else by reference (doesn't restate Why or re-derive contract):

1. Architectural Meaning

What does this responsibility mean inside our system?

2. Implementation

How will we build it — design, data changes, migration?

3. Verification & Proof

How will we prove it works, including failure cases?

4. Ranged Estimation

What's our estimate range, and what would change it?
