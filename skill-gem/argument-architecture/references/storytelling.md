# Making It Concrete: P.A.S.T.

Logic gets a decision approved. Concreteness gets it remembered, repeated by people who weren't in the room, and acted on after the meeting ends. P.A.S.T. — Place, Action, Speech, Thoughts — is the craft technique for getting there.

It is a craft heuristic, not an empirical finding. It earns its place by working, and it should be applied where it works rather than everywhere.

## The four elements

**Place.** Establish the physical location fast, so a picture starts forming. One clause is usually enough: "In the Tuesday support triage." Not a paragraph of setting.

**Action.** Start as close to the critical moment as possible. Cut the run-up. The audience does not need the background that led to the moment; they need the moment.

**Speech (outer dialogue).** Quote people directly instead of summarizing them.
- Summary: "Judy was satisfied with our work."
- Speech: "Judy called and said, 'Exceptional job — without your help we'd never have gotten there.'"

Summaries are clinical and drain the energy out of a moment. A direct quote puts the audience in the room and lets them hear the personality and the conflict.

**Thoughts (inner dialogue).** Share the raw internal monologue, not a named emotion.
- Naming the emotion: "I was worried the client would reject our pricing."
- Thoughts: "I stared at the screen and thought, 'if she sees this number she's going to laugh me out of the room.'"

Naming an emotion still filters the experience and holds the audience at arm's length. The unfiltered thought is what creates connection, because it's the thing people recognize from their own heads.

## Where each element attaches to the argument

This mapping matters more than it looks. Attaching the wrong element to the wrong component produces an artifact that feels compelling and argues badly.

### Place and Action → the grounds, as illustration

Open the evidence section with one scene rather than a summary of the problem:

> "In Tuesday's triage queue, a beta user tapped Register. The screen went white. He waited eight seconds, then closed the app."

**The scene illustrates the chain. It does not replace it.** This is the failure to guard against hardest, because a vivid anecdote is *more persuasive* than aggregate data while being *less evidentiary* — one user is an instance, not a pattern. A single scene supported by no chain is exactly how confident people talk themselves and their audiences into bad decisions.

The working combination is: one scene to make it real, then the chain to make it true. If the user has a scene but no chain, send them back to step 2. If they have a chain and no scene, the argument is sound but forgettable.

### Speech → grounds and stakes, *not* the warrant

The first source document maps Speech onto the warrant, with an example like *"our engineering lead said, 'if a user waits more than three seconds, they're gone.'"*

Don't do that. That quote is a colleague asserting a rule, and an assertion doesn't become backing by being said out loud in someone's voice. It's the jargon problem in better clothing — the warrant still hasn't been justified, it's just been made vivid, which makes the gap harder to notice rather than easier. If the three-second rule is load-bearing, it needs the study, the funnel data, or the prior test.

Speech works well for two other things:

- **Grounds** — what a customer, user, or stakeholder actually said *is* evidence of their experience. "Three separate users wrote 'I thought it was broken'" is data.
- **Stakes** — a decision-maker's own words about what they care about, quoted back, is the most efficient way to connect your claim to their priorities.

### Thoughts → the rebuttal and qualifier

This is the strongest of the mappings, and it's worth using deliberately.

Your remembered doubt is almost always where the real weakness lives. You already knew it at the time; you just moved past it. Recovering the thought recovers the objection:

> "I sat there and thought, 'if the primary database goes down mid-migration, none of this matters.'"

That thought *is* the rebuttal, and it hands you the qualifier: "Provided the primary stays up through Phase 1." Naming your own doubt before the audience finds it is the single strongest credibility move available, because it demonstrates you've already been where they're going.

## Two constraints that keep this honest

**Quotes must be real.** Storytelling coaching often permits reconstructing plausible dialogue — "use the words that would realistically have been used." That is fine on a stage telling a personal story. It is not fine in a work artifact where the quote is attributed to a named colleague who may be in the room, on the thread, or forwarded the deck.

The rule for work: verbatim, or don't attribute. If the wording is approximate, mark it — "roughly, she said…" or drop the name. A quote someone doesn't recognize as theirs destroys the argument and some of the relationship.

**Calibrate Thoughts to the audience.** Vulnerability is not universally an asset. It plays well in an article, a retrospective, an all-hands, or with a team that knows you. It can misfire when presenting upward to a skeptical stakeholder deciding whether to trust your judgment, in an adversarial setting, or in a culture that reads disclosed doubt as lack of conviction.

When it doesn't fit, keep the *function* and drop the *form*: state the failure condition flatly as a risk rather than as an internal monologue. The qualifier still gets set — it just doesn't get narrated.

## Dosage

One scene per artifact, usually — placed where the argument turns. Decks tolerate a scene in the opening or at the grounds. Articles can carry more. Memos and business cases carry the least; a scene in a two-page recommendation memo is often one paragraph and no more.

If every section has a scene, none of them land, and the artifact reads as padded. Concreteness is a spotlight, and a spotlight pointed everywhere is just lighting.
