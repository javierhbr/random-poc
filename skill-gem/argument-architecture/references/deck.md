# Placement: Presentation Decks

A deck is the medium where a presenter is standing there. That changes everything about placement: the slides carry only what must survive without you, and you carry the rest.

**Slides hold the claim and the grounds. The other four components are Q&A armor.**

## Slide-by-slide

**Slide 1 — the claim.** Not "Q4 Marketing Review." The actual ask, as a sentence. Title-as-topic is the single most common way a deck loses the room before it starts, because it signals that no decision is being requested and the audience settles into passive mode.

**Slides 2–4 — the grounds, as the chain.** One link per slide, or one slide with the chain laid out visually. Resist the instinct to lead with the headline number and back-fill; the chain is what makes the number unarguable.

**Final slide — the qualifier, framed as scope.** "This assumes search inventory holds near current prices. Above $2.00 CPC the case weakens." Ending on honest scope reads as confidence, not hedging, and it preempts the exception-hunting that kills decks in the last two minutes.

**Appendix — the backing.** Prepared, not presented. You pull it up only when challenged, which is far more persuasive than pre-emptively defending a warrant nobody questioned.

**No slide — the warrant.** It lives in your mouth, in the transition between grounds and claim. Say it aloud in your rehearsal so it comes out cleanly under pressure.

**No slide — the rebuttals.** Prepared answers, held in reserve.

## Deck-specific notes

**On slide count.** Every slide should be a link in the chain or the claim itself. If a slide is context, background, or "so you understand the landscape," it belongs in the pre-read or nowhere. Executives read ahead and interrupt; a lean deck survives being jumped around in, a padded one doesn't.

**On the interruption problem.** Assume you will not present linearly. Someone will jump to slide 6 or ask the hardest question during slide 2. Build so that any single slide can be defended standing alone, and so that you can state the whole argument in thirty seconds if the meeting collapses to that.

**On the escalating counterargument.** The rebuttal move worth knowing is taking an objection's own logic to its endpoint and pricing it: *"If we wait for the seasonal theory to confirm, that's two more quarters at current CAC — about $400K. Happy to do that if we're comfortable with the number."* This works only when the opposing position has an unacknowledged cost. It fails, and reads as combative, against a good-faith objection or a more senior person. Use it once at most.

**On the scene.** One, at the opening or at the head of the grounds. A deck's slide is a poor container for narrative — the scene belongs in what you *say* over a simple slide, not in text on it. Bullet-pointing a story kills it. If the scene involves a quoted stakeholder who is in the room, get the quote right or don't name them.

**On pre-reads.** If the deck has a written pre-read, the pre-read carries the warrant and backing explicitly (nobody's there to speak them) and the deck stays lean. See `document.md` for how to structure it.
