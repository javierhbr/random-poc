# Placement: Memos, Proposals, Business Cases, Strategy Documents

A document is read without you in the room. Nobody can ask you what you meant, and it will be forwarded to people you didn't write it for. Both facts change placement.

**Everything must be on the page. The warrant especially — this is the main difference from a deck.**

## Structure

**Open with the claim.** First sentence or a bolded recommendation line at the top. Business readers scan; a claim buried under context gets missed by exactly the person whose approval you need. Include the number, date, or owner if the decision has one.

**State the warrant early, right after the claim.** In a deck you'd speak it. Here it has to be written, and written before the evidence rather than after, because it tells the reader what the evidence is *for*. One or two sentences: "Channels that degrade structurally don't recover on their own; the reallocation question is when, not whether."

**Then the grounds, as the chain.** Each link checkable. Documents tolerate more detail than slides, so this is where the step-by-step actually belongs — but keep it a chain, not a data dump.

**Backing inline or footnoted.** Unlike a deck, you can't wait to be challenged, because the challenge arrives as a forwarded email three days later with your VP copied. Cite the precedent, the pilot data, the prior audit at the point where the warrant needs support.

**Rebuttals in their own section, steelmanned.** Head it "Objections and responses" or "What could go wrong." Stating the strongest version of the counterargument and answering it is the highest-credibility move available in written form — and it preempts the reader's own objection arriving unanswered while they read alone.

**Close on the qualifier and the ask.** Scope, then the specific decision requested and by when.

## Document-specific notes

**On the forwarding problem.** Write for a reader two steps removed who has none of your context and wasn't in any of the prior conversations. Anything that depends on shared history — an internal nickname, an unstated prior decision, "as discussed" — should be spelled out. This is not padding; it's what keeps the argument intact when it travels.

**On length and the executive summary.** If the document runs past two pages, open with a summary that contains claim, warrant, and qualifier in under 150 words. If you can't compress those three into 150 words, the argument is still muddy — go back to step 1. Grounds can be summarized loosely; the other three cannot.

**On tone in the rebuttal section.** Written objections read harsher than spoken ones. State the counterargument neutrally and answer it on the merits. The escalating cost move that works verbally in a meeting ("if we wait, that's $400K") reads as aggressive on the page — state the number as a tradeoff rather than a challenge.

**On the scene.** Memos carry the least narrative of the three formats — usually one short paragraph, placed at the head of the grounds, and none at all in a document under a page. The executive summary stays scene-free; it exists to be skimmed. Be especially careful with quoted colleagues here, since the document will be forwarded and the quoted person may end up reading it.

**On decision documents specifically.** If the document exists to get a decision made in a meeting, name the decision, the options considered, and your recommendation in the first paragraph. Readers who agree stop there, which is the point.
