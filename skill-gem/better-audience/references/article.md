# Placement: Articles, Blog Posts, External Essays

An article has no shared context, no authority from your role, and an audience that can stop reading at any sentence. Placement adapts to all three.

**The warrant is usually the whole piece. The claim often arrives late.**

## Structure

**Open by earning attention, not by stating the claim.** Unlike a memo, leading with the recommendation often fails — a reader with no stake in your conclusion has no reason to continue. Open with the tension, the surprising observation, or the problem the reader already recognizes. The claim can wait.

**The claim, once the reader is invested.** Still a single clear sentence, still specific. Vagueness that a colleague would forgive because they know your context will simply lose an external reader.

**The warrant gets the most space.** In a memo the warrant is a sentence; here it's frequently the substance of the article. Internal readers grant you shared premises. External readers grant nothing. If your argument depends on "structural decline doesn't self-correct," you have to establish that, not assert it — and doing so well is usually what makes the piece worth reading.

**Grounds as illustration, not exhaustive proof.** A memo's chain of five metrics becomes, in an article, one worked example the reader can hold onto. Concrete beats comprehensive. The chain still has to be *sound* — you just don't show every link.

**Backing as visible citation.** Link it. External credibility comes from checkable sources, and readers who check are the ones most likely to share.

**Rebuttals woven through, steelmanned hard.** Address the strongest objection in the body, at the point where an informed reader would raise it. An unaddressed obvious counterargument is the fastest way to lose a smart reader — they stop reading and assume you don't know the field.

**Qualifier stated plainly, usually near the end.** Naming the limits of your own argument is the strongest credibility signal available in public writing. Overclaiming is what gets a piece dismissed by the people you most wanted to reach.

## Article-specific notes

**On the hostile-reader test.** Assume a knowledgeable reader who disagrees. They are the audience that matters — agreeable readers were already convinced, and hostile-but-fair readers are the ones who spread the piece if it holds up. Every unaddressed weak point is where they stop.

**On the fabricated-statistic risk.** Articles get fact-checked by strangers, and a single invented citation discredits everything around it. Verify every number and every attributed claim before publishing. If a compelling statistic can't be traced to a primary source, cut it — the argument is stronger without it than with a claim that collapses under a search. (This is exactly how the source material behind this skill fell apart. See the Provenance section in SKILL.md.)

**On the escalating counterargument.** The cost-of-inaction move works in public writing only when the cost is genuinely quantified and not rhetorical. Readers detect the rhetorical version immediately and it reads as manipulation.

**On voice.** Toulmin structure should be invisible in a finished article. If a reader can see the scaffolding — if it reads like a five-paragraph essay with the joints exposed — the structure has failed at its job, which is to make the conclusion feel inevitable rather than assembled.
